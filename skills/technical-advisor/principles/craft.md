# Craft: how much design now, and what is left behind?

The claims about the share of work that goes into design, and about what a change leaves behind it. Whether a change happens now is never answered here, and the roster, `../../advisors/SKILL.md`, holds the test for a design move during an open bet.

## Strategic over tactical

**A steady share of the work goes into keeping the design good, and a deep recast is recognised when those small steps make it visible, not set out for.** Tactical programming gets the feature out and leaves a little mess each time, and the mess compounds; strategic programming invests a small, steady share, a tenth to a fifth, and the investment pays back within months. The share is small and continuous, not a redesign. Modest refinements are what set a deep recast up, and it is recognised when they make it visible; a change whose aim is to find the deep model rather than to improve the one at hand is not one of them. An early product feels the strongest pull toward the tactical and gains least from it: good or bad design shows within months, so the shortcut may not even speed the first release, and a codebase gone to tangle is almost never recovered. An increment of the work is an abstraction, not a feature: no abstraction is made before a feature needs one, and when a change makes one, its interface is designed whole then rather than grown a piece per feature. Check: what does this change make cheaper for the next one, what does it leave to compound, and is it improving the model at hand or searching for a deeper one with nothing pulling it? Sources: Ousterhout, chapter 3, Working Code Isn't Enough; chapter 16, Modifying Existing Code (16.1 Stay strategic); Evans, chapter 8, Breakthrough (Opportunities; Focus on Basics); Ousterhout, chapter 19, Software Trends (19.2 Agile development; 19.4 Test-driven development).

## A rule, not a picture

**A decision about the shape of the whole is written as a rule every change applies where it lands, where a kind of thing goes, how two parts connect, what a part may know, and never as a target layout the changes fill in.** A picture of the finished whole is too precise about the whole, which it cannot foresee, and not precise enough about the connections each change must make, so it is abandoned at the first change it did not foresee or followed into a shape fitted to nothing; a rule applied at each change grows an order fitted to what actually arrived, which is the large-scale structure in Evans's word, read off the code after the changes and never drawn ahead of them. Check: does this change fill in a slot of a layout drawn ahead of it, and if it settles something about the whole, is that written as a rule the next change can apply where it lands? Source: Evans, chapter 17, Bringing the Strategy Together (Why Master Plans Fail; What is Essential for Strategic Design Decisions to be Effective?, Must Allow Evolution).

## Justified by the domain, never by the technique

**A design move in the domain part is justified by what the model then says of the domain, in words the people who know it would use, and never by the technique it shows.** A suppler shape, a pattern, a declarative style, each earns its place only where it cuts to something of the domain; one that displays skill and says nothing new of the domain is left out however elegant, and a deeper model that the people who know the domain would not take up to talk with is not deeper, whatever its form. So the spec of a change that reshapes the domain part states, in the domain's words, what the model says after it that it could not say before, and a change that cannot fill that line is a code change kept small, or is dropped. A pattern that fits is hard to better, but a problem is never forced into one where a shape of its own would be cleaner, and more patterns do not make a better design. Check: what does the model say of the domain after this change that it could not before, in the domain's words, and would the people who know the domain use it to talk? Sources: Evans, chapter 13, Refactoring Toward Deeper Insight (Timing, the closing cautions; Exploration Teams, Exercising the Ubiquitous Language); Ousterhout, chapter 19, Software Trends (19.5 Design patterns).

## A principle is judged by the complexity it removes

**A principle is followed where it makes the code easier to understand or to change, and set aside, aloud, where it does not.** Every rule has exceptions and every principle pushed to its end goes wrong; a good design balances ideas that pull against each other, and the aim under all of them, less complexity, counts for more than any one. A shape that holds a principle and leaves the code harder to work in has misread it, and a change that sets a principle aside says which one and why. Check: does any shape in this change hold a principle at the cost of making the code harder to understand or to change, and where a principle is set aside, does the spec say which and why? Sources: Ousterhout, Preface; chapter 1, Introduction (1.1 How to use this book).

## Design it twice

**Two shapes are sketched before one is chosen.** The second sketch is cheap, and comparing the two shows what the first one hid, even for small things. The second is made as unlike the first as it can be, even where only one seems reasonable, and the two are weighed first by how easily the code that calls them can use them. Where neither is good, the work each leaves to its callers points to a third, and the pick may combine features of both. A few changes the code may plausibly need are one way to weigh the two, and nothing is built for them. Check: what was the other shape, how unlike the chosen one was it, and why not it, by what each would leave its callers to do? Sources: Ousterhout, chapter 11, Design It Twice; chapter 3, Working Code Isn't Enough (3.2 Strategic programming).

## Tracer bullets, not prototypes

**A thin, working, end-to-end path is kept and grown; a prototype is thrown away.** A tracer bullet is a working path through every layer that the next change widens; a prototype is built to learn one thing and never shipped. Which one is being built is known before it starts. The path runs through the core domain, however simple its first cut, since a path through a generic part proves only the plumbing. Check: is this a path that will be kept, or a throwaway, and is it known which? Sources: Hunt and Thomas, topic 12, Tracer Bullets; topic 13, Prototypes and Post-it Notes; Evans, chapter 15, Distillation (Generic Subdomains, Project Risk Management).

## The model on its own first

**A change that recasts the model is run on its own and shown before it reaches a store or a screen; a small change goes straight in.** The new or changed part of the model is written as domain code alone and run on sample data, with no store and no screen, so it can be shown to the people who know the domain and corrected while correction is cheap; the store and the screen follow once the model has held. It is a tool reached for when the change is big enough to be worth it, not a rite for every change. Check: would this change's model be cheaper to correct if it were run and shown before its store and screen exist? Source: Evans, chapter 1, Crunching Knowledge, the prototype, and Why It Worked, binding model and implementation.

## The fault is seated before it is fixed

**A problem in the domain part is placed in the code or in the model before its fix is shaped, and code that reads cleanly is no evidence that the model holds.** An awkwardness in the code may have its root in the model, a concept missing or a relation wrong, and a standard transformation of the code, a rename, an extraction, a move, leaves that root where it was for the next change to find again; so the first question of a fix is where the fault sits, and a fault in the model gets a model change. The sign need not be in the code at all: code that reads well while its words have drifted from the people who know the domain, a requirement that does not fit in naturally, or a learning that shows a plainer model, is the same kind of fault and its fix takes the same shape. Check: is the root of the problem this change fixes in the code or in the model, does the fix sit where the root is, and has a clean-reading part been taken for a sound one? Source: Evans, chapter 13, Refactoring Toward Deeper Insight (Initiation; Timing, the three conditions read as signs).

## A shared piece is fixed in the piece

**A fault or a misfit found on one screen in a piece other screens share is acted on in the piece, changed for every use, given a named variation or replaced by another piece, and never patched or overridden on the one screen.** A problem is seen where a piece is used, and is often the piece's wherever it is used; an override on the one screen fixes that screen and leaves the fault in the others, and a run of such one-off changes leaves the shared pieces no longer shared. So the first question of a fix found on a screen is whether the fault is the screen's or the piece's, and a fault of the piece is fixed once, where every use gets it. Check: does this change fix on one screen a fault of a piece other screens share, or override a shared piece where the piece, or a named variation of it, should change? Source: Brad Frost, Atomic Design (2016), chapter 5, Maintaining Design Systems (Changing minds, once again, What is it we're making again?; Make it adaptable, Making changes to patterns), `reference/atomic-design/chapter-5.md`.

## Patch or recast

**A run of requirements that each arrive as a surprise and land as a special case is a symptom of a wrong model, and the next change recasts rather than patches.** When every new rule comes as an unexpected branch, a subtype or a correction on the same thing, and an inconsistency survives ever more careful code, the fault is in how the model ties its things together, not in the cases. The third such patch on one thing is the sign; the shape is then the model walked afresh with the people who know the domain, from the scenarios the patches were covering. Check: is this change another special case on a thing that has had two already, or an inconsistency chased with more code, and if so, what would the model look like if it held the scenario plainly? Source: Evans, chapter 8, Breakthrough (A Decent Model, and Yet; The Breakthrough).

## The smallest thing that works

**The smallest shape that still holds the principle, and nothing for a need only imagined.** A version that does its job well enough to be measured beats a perfect one that is not out yet, and a part built for a presumed need costs its building, its carrying and the chance it was wrong. The project's instructions (`AGENTS.md` or `CLAUDE.md`) state the building rules that rest on this claim. Small is counted in what a reader must know to work in the shape, not in its lines: a longer shape that leaves less to know is the smaller one. Check: what in this change does no bet pull, and can it come out? Sources: Hunt and Thomas, topic 5, Good-Enough Software; Fowler, Yagni; Ousterhout, chapter 2, The Nature of Complexity (2.2 Symptoms of complexity).

## Naturally efficient

**Of two shapes equally simple, the one that does less costly work is chosen, and complexity is added for speed only where it stays hidden or the need is already known.** Ignoring cost everywhere spreads inefficiency so thin that no single fix helps later, "death by a thousand cuts", and tuning every line slows the work and adds complexity that often buys nothing. The middle way knows which operations are expensive, a round trip over the network, a read or a write to storage, an allocation that could be avoided, and picks the cheap one where it is as clean; a faster shape that would complicate an interface or much of the implementation starts as the simpler one unless there is clear evidence beforehand that the speed will matter. Simple code is usually the fast code as well, since a deep module does more per call and a special case defined away is a check never run. Check: does this change pick the costlier of two equally clean shapes, and does any complexity it adds for speed reach an interface without evidence that the speed is needed? Source: Ousterhout, chapter 20, Designing for Performance (20.1 How to think about performance; 20.5 Conclusion).

## Measured before tuned

**Where a change is made for speed, it starts from a measurement that places where the time goes, and it stays only if a measurement after it shows the gain.** Intuition about what is slow is unreliable, an experienced one too, and a change made on it adds complexity where none was needed. A measurement of the whole says only that it is slow; the one that guides a change goes deep enough to find the few places the time is spent, and it is the baseline the change is measured against afterwards. A change with no measurable gain is backed out unless it left the code simpler. Where measured code must still run faster and no fundamental fix, a cache or a different algorithm, is at hand, it is redesigned around its critical path, the least code the common case must run: one test at its start sends every special case to code of its own, and shallow layers on the path go. Check: for each part of this change made for speed, what measurement placed it, and what measurement after it shows the gain? Source: Ousterhout, chapter 20, Designing for Performance (20.2 Measure before modifying; 20.3 Design around the critical path; 20.4 An example: RAMCloud Buffers).

## Fine where it matters

**The model is fine-grained only in its core domain, the part distinctive to what the app does for its users, where change is most common, and plain everywhere else.** A deep model is not evenly detailed: the part users' value turns on, and the part that changes most, gets the distinctions, the types and the suppleness, since that is where flexibility pays back; the rest is held as simply as it can be and still be right, since detail there is weight carried on every change and nothing gained. The core domain, in Evans's word, is the part of the model no app of another kind would hold and the part the app is worth building for; it is chosen by what the app is for, never by what is interesting, well-defined or thick in the code, and it is redrawn as insight deepens, since what seemed central at first may prove supporting. A definable problem beside the core, a unit, a time, a chart of who reports to whom, draws craft because it can be understood without the domain, and craft spent there is taken from the core; such a part gets the plainest shape that is right. So a new thing's grain is decided by where it sits: in the core it gets types and rules of its own, beside it it stays as a few plain fields on the thing that holds it. Check: does the thing this change adds sit in the core domain or beside it, is its grain fine there and plain here, and is the craft in this change drawn by the core or by a definable problem beside it? Sources: Evans, chapter 13, Refactoring Toward Deeper Insight (A Design for Developers); Evans, chapter 15, Distillation (Core Domain; Choosing the Core; Generic Subdomains, A Tale of Two Time Zones; Deep Models Distill).

## Choose the road whole

**A new surface aims to pick its road knowingly, rules in the screen or rules in a model, and to stay off the halfway shape.** For a small thing that is mostly entry and display with few rules, rules in the screen is fast and honest, and it has no graceful path to richer behaviour: it grows only by more small screens and is left by replacement, never by migration. A thing with ambition starts simple too, and keeps its model apart from its first step. The costly shape is the middle, heavy infrastructure around a simple thing, or a model begun and not carried through. This is an aim to hold in mind at a new surface, not a rule for every change. Check: which road has this surface chosen, and is this change on it rather than halfway between? Source: Evans, chapter 4, Isolating the Domain, Smart UI Anti-Pattern.

## No broken windows

**Rot is fixed or written down, never left unmarked.** One tolerated bit of rot invites more; each is fixed in the version, or boarded up visibly in the repo's own files, where it is seen and not silently tolerated. Each bit of complexity looks too small to matter, which is how it piles up, and once piled up it is hard to clear, since removing any one bit gains little. Complexity a change adds without need is fixed in that change, even where it is marked; what may be boarded up is rot the change finds in code it did not make, and a shape the store or the framework forces is a compromise, not rot. Check: does this change add complexity it did not need, is anything left as a broken window, and if so, is it written in the repo's own files? Sources: Hunt and Thomas, topic 3, Software Entropy; Ousterhout, chapter 2, The Nature of Complexity (2.4 Complexity is incremental); chapter 3, Working Code Isn't Enough (3.5 Conclusion).

## A compromise is marked with what it bends

**A shape forced on the model by its store, its framework or the code as it stands is taken knowingly, confined to where it is forced, and marked in the code and the spec as a compromise beside the shape the model would have chosen, so no reader takes it for the model.** A compromise is not rot: it is the best shape available that is still not the model's, and the work moves on rather than stalling on it or forgetting it. Confined, it stays where the store, the framework or the script forces it; marked, it carries the cleaner shape beside it, so a later reader sees a bend and not the model. Check: does any shape in this change exist for the store's, the framework's or the script's sake rather than the model's, and if so is it marked as such, with the shape it bends from? Source: Evans, chapter 11, Applying Analysis Patterns (Example: Earning Interest with Accounts, the class diagram after the implementation and its note; Continuing Example: Insight into the Nightly Batch, the closing paragraphs).

## Refactor early, test early

**Small, frequent changes under the net that runs now.** The net is the tests that can be run right now, not the ones planned; and a change to existing code stays strategic, leaving the code as if it had been designed this way from the start, rather than patched. Where that shape costs more than the change can carry, the shape sought is one nearly as clean that fits, not the quick fix. Without a net that catches what a structural change breaks, such changes are avoided and each change kept as small as it can be, so complexity accumulates and design mistakes stay in place. Check: did the net run before the push, and does this change leave the code as if designed this way from the start? Sources: Hunt and Thomas, topic 40, Refactoring; topic 41, Test to Code; Ousterhout, chapter 16, Modifying Existing Code (16.1 Stay strategic); chapter 19, Software Trends (19.3 Unit tests).

## The test before the maker

**A thing that makes or arranges other things takes the rule its output must satisfy, the rule is written and run as a check before the maker exists, and the first maker that passes it is kept.** The maker's interface says what its output satisfies, not how; the same rule proves the output; a crude maker that satisfies the rule lets the rest proceed and is replaced when it must be. Check: does this change's maker take a declared rule, was the rule a check before the maker was built, and does the first maker pass it? Source: Evans, chapter 9, Making Implicit Concepts Explicit (Building to Order, Generating; Example: Chemical Warehouse Packer; Clearing Development Logjams with Working Prototypes).

## A double only where the real thing is awkward

**A test runs the real thing and reads the state it leaves; a test double, in Fowler's word, stands in only for a collaborator that is awkward to use in a test, a store, a network, a clock, and it is read for the state it holds after the call, verified by the calls it received only where its state cannot show what it did.** The kinds are the five Fowler takes from Meszaros: a dummy is passed and never used, a fake works but takes a shortcut unfit for use, a stub answers what the test programmed into it, a spy is a stub that also records what it was sent, and a mock is set up with the calls it expects and fails when they differ. Only the mock verifies behaviour, and a test that verifies behaviour is bound to how the thing under test talks to its neighbours, so it breaks on a refactor that leaves every result the same and passes on a wrong expectation that masks a fault, while a test on the real thing is also a small test of the pair and catches what each one's own test misses. A double is reached for case by case where the real thing is awkward and never as a rule for every neighbour with behaviour; a thing whose state cannot show what it did, a cache, is the one case for checking calls. Check: does any test in this change put a double in for a thing that was easy to use real, and does any double verify the calls it received where the state it was left in would say the same? Sources: Fowler, Test Double; Fowler, Mocks Aren't Stubs (The Difference Between Mocks and Stubs; Classical and Mockist Testing; Coupling Tests to Implementations).

## A recast is one change

**A recast of the model with no working stopping point between its steps is one change, specified whole with its cost and what it pays back.** Most changes go in small steps under the net; a recast that would leave parts of the app disabled at every midpoint is not cut into them and shipped half-made. Its spec says how long it takes to get back to what works today, whether the problems it fixes could be fixed without it, and what the same change would cost later with users to carry. Whether and when it then happens is not a question of shape. Check: does this change have a working stopping point between its steps, and if not, does its spec state the cost to get back to current function, the alternative, and what it pays back? Source: Evans, chapter 8, Breakthrough (A Sobering Decision; The Payoff).

## Replace through a seam

**A working part is replaced by growing its successor beside it behind a seam, a piece at a time, the old part serving every piece not yet moved, a strangler fig in Fowler's word, and never by a second build that sets out to do all the old one did.** A replacement built whole must first learn what the old part does in every detail, much of which is not wanted and so is built as waste, and nothing of it holds until all of it does; a piece moved behind a seam is small, holds on its own, and teaches what the next piece will cost. A seam is a place where a call into the old part can be switched to the new without editing the old part, cut where one need the app serves can be lifted out whole, and the code that exists only so old and new can run together is transitional, marked as such where it sits and gone when the last piece has moved. Where no seam can be cut and no piece can hold alone, the replacement is a recast and is specified as one. Check: does this change rebuild a working part whole where a seam would let a piece move alone, is each piece cut to what is wanted rather than to what the old part did, and is every line that exists only for the two to coexist marked transitional? Source: Fowler, Strangler Fig Application.

## A stalled search is cut to one aspect

**A search for a better model that will not settle is too wide, and is cut to one aspect of the model taken alone, the rest left as it is.** A model refinement that is going to settle does so in a few short sittings, with the scenarios walked and the people who know the domain reading it; one that drags is not nearly done but over-scoped, and pressing on widens it further. The shape is then a smaller question, one concept, one relation, one operation, settled and coded on its own with the rest named as what waits; the next aspect is a change of its own. Check: if this model change has not settled, which one aspect of it could be settled and coded alone, and what is then named as waiting? Source: Evans, chapter 13, Refactoring Toward Deeper Insight (Exploration Teams, Scope and sleep).

## Automate the repeatable

**Anything run twice gets a script.** A step done by hand is a step that will one day be skipped or done differently; the build, the check, the export and the record are scripts. Check: does this change add a step done by hand that will be done again? Source: Hunt and Thomas, topic 51, Pragmatic Starter Kit.

## Know why it works

**Code that works for a reason nobody can give is not working yet.** If no one can say why something works, no one can keep it working or tell when it has stopped; a result that appears by coincidence is tested until the reason is known. A fix is known to work when a check that failed because of the fault passes after it; a check written only after the fix may never have met the fault. Check: can every behaviour this change relies on be explained? Sources: Hunt and Thomas, topic 38, Programming by Coincidence; Ousterhout, chapter 19, Software Trends (19.4 Test-driven development).
