# Growth: how does the product grow once users keep using it?

The claims about growth: where it comes from, how it is measured, and what it hides. They wait on value: until users keep coming back, no experiment is about growth, and the first claim says what is done with growth's signs before then.

## Growth waits on value, and its first signs are noted

**Growth is tested only once value is shown, but its first signs show on a very few users: where an early user brings another, it is written down beside the value experiment's number, never taken as its measure.** Ries's first experiment for a model of growth counts, among a dozen early adopters, how many recruit a colleague, alongside how many come back. If the model needs a certain share of users to bring others, that share can be seen long before there are many users, and noting it costs the value experiment nothing. Check: has anyone among the first users brought someone, and is it written down without being read as the result? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down).

## The push waits for the product to work

**A marketing push, a launch or a campaign waits until the product has solved the problem for its early users, because promoting it sooner spends attention on something not yet worth finding.** In Ries's example, the team held off the planned launch until early users stopped running into a missing step, against a schedule set months before, and iterating before the campaign did far better than launching on the date. Check: is this work meant to bring new users to the product before the users it has keep coming back? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (An experiment is a product).

## Sustainable growth comes from past customers

**Sustainable growth means new customers come from the actions of past customers; a one-time surge from a stunt or from spending invested capital is not growth.** Ries names four ways past customers bring new ones: word of mouth, exposure as a side effect of use, advertising paid for out of revenue, and repeat purchase or use. These feed the loops he calls engines of growth. Check: does this bring new users through what existing users do, or through a one-time push that stops when the push stops? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (Where does growth come from?).

## Growth explained by value

**Growth is judged by its cause: growth from continual fund-raising and paid advertising without a product that creates value is success theater, the appearance of success.** Why a product grows matters, because many kinds of growth destroy value, and innovation accounting exists partly to tell such false successes from true ones. Check: if users are increasing, what about the product's value explains it? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Value and growth); chapter 7, Measure (Optimization versus learning).

## Each engine has its own number

**What to measure follows from how the product is meant to grow: each engine of growth has its own measure, retention against churn for the sticky engine, the viral coefficient for the viral engine, and what a customer is worth against what one costs to win for the paid engine.** A sticky product grows by the rate of compounding, new customers minus those lost; a viral one grows only with a coefficient above one; a paid one grows by the margin between a customer's worth and the cost of acquiring one. The leaps of faith become the tuning variables of that engine, and a measure that suits the value hypothesis can say little about it. Check: which engine is this product assumed to grow by, and does this work move that engine's number? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 7, Measure (An accountability framework that works across industries); chapter 10, Grow (The sticky engine of growth; The viral engine of growth; The paid engine of growth).

## One engine at a time

**A new product focuses on one engine of growth at a time, and turns to another only after pursuing the first thoroughly.** More than one engine can run in a business, but successful ventures specialize in one, and a dashboard of all three breeds confusion. Most founders already hold a leap-of-faith assumption about which engine will work; time with customers suggests one if not. Check: which single engine is this growth work for, and has it been pursued thoroughly before another is tried? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (A technical caveat).

## Retention before acquisition

**Where a product depends on repeat use and growth stalls, the lever is retention, not more acquisition.** Ries shows a product whose healthy acquisition was cancelled by equal churn: more marketing would have poured customers into the same leak, while making the product more engaging for those already using it is what restarted growth. Totals of customers hide this. Check: before more users are sought, are those already using the product staying? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (The sticky engine of growth).

## Early adopters, then the mainstream

**A new product reaches early adopters before the mainstream, and the two want different things: early adopters accept, even prefer, an incomplete product and fill its gaps with imagination, while mainstream customers are far less forgiving, so growth among early adopters does not carry over without substantial new work.** Polish beyond what early adopters ask for is waste, but every engine of growth is tied to a particular set of customers and eventually exhausts them, and the slowdown can take long enough to be mistaken for success. When the early adopters run out, growth stalls unless the product meets the mainstream's needs, often through a pivot to a new customer segment. Check: which set of customers is this growth drawn from, and what would show it is running out? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (Why first products aren't meant to be perfect; The concierge minimum viable product); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Failure to pivot); chapter 10, Grow (When engines run out).

## Value capture is part of the product

**How value is captured is part of the product's hypothesis, not a feature to add or remove later, and changing it reaches the product, the business and its marketing.** Treating payment as a module bolted on afterwards hides an assumption that shapes everything else. Check: how would the value this creates be captured, and what does that assume about the product? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Value capture pivot).
