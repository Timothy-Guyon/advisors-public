# Experiments: what is the smallest thing that tests it, and what does it leave out?

The claims about how an experiment is designed before it runs: the question it answers, how small it is, who it reaches, and what it holds back.

## One question above all

**The first experiment is a small, simple product built to answer one question above all, and starting small does not shrink the vision.** Ries's example tests whether demand exists before building any of the operations the full vision would need; the large vision came later, built on what the small test showed. Waiting to test the whole vision at once is how ventures spend everything before learning anything. Check: what is the one question this experiment answers, and what in it serves the vision rather than that question? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (Think big, start small; For long-term change, experiment immediately).

## Plan in reverse

**The loop runs build, measure, learn, but it is planned in reverse: what must be learned, then what must be measured to know it, then the smallest product that runs that experiment.** That is why a minimum viable product carries work beyond its features: it has to be able to measure its own effect. Starting from what can be built inverts the loop and yields features in search of a question, and a feature built outside an experiment saves time now and costs more later, when someone has to find out whether it mattered. Check: were the learning and its measure decided before what to build, and does the build carry the measure from the start? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 7, Measure (Kanban); chapter 9, Batch (Pull, don't push).

## Write the prediction first

**An experiment begins with a prediction, written before it runs, that a result can prove wrong, and every result it could give is tied to a verdict in advance.** A team that launches to see what happens always succeeds at seeing something and cannot tell success from failure; with an unclear hypothesis complete failure is almost impossible, and without failure nothing forces a change. Where the reading was not agreed before the test, each side reads the data its own way and the decision falls back to the most plausible argument. A true experiment is easy to call a success or a failure, because its measure either moves or does not. Check: what result, written before this experiment runs, would show the prediction wrong, and is every other result already tied to a verdict? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (opening; From alchemy to science); chapter 7, Measure (Optimization versus learning); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots; Pivots require courage); chapter 12, Innovate (Protecting the parent organization; Creating an innovation sandbox).

## The minimum viable product

**A minimum viable product is the version that completes one full turn of build, measure and learn with the least effort and time, which is not always the version that builds the least.** Its goal is to begin learning, not to end it, and unlike a prototype it tests the fundamental hypotheses of the business, not only design or technical ones. It ranges from a smoke test to an early product with problems and missing features, and choosing among them takes judgment. Small is counted in the effort and time to a user's result: a version done by hand that reaches a result sooner is smaller than a build that makes users wait for it. Check: of the versions that would test this, which one reaches a user's result with the least effort and time? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 6, Test (opening; Why first products aren't meant to be perfect).

## Cut it until the answer would change

**An experiment is as small in what it builds and in whom it reaches as its question allows, and the smallest may build nothing at all.** Ries asks whether half as much, a third, a single case, or nothing built would have given the same learning; the effort beyond that point was waste. Most makers badly overestimate how many features a first version needs, and when in doubt, it is simplified. The same holds for reach: a narrow first group of customers or a single use gives a readable result for little cost. Check: would this experiment teach the same thing with half of it, with one case, with fewer users, or by offering it before anything is built? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Value vs. waste); chapter 4, Experiment (A Lean Startup in government?); chapter 6, Test (Why first products aren't meant to be perfect).

## Early adopters, not the average customer

**The first experiment goes to early adopters, the customers who feel the need most acutely, not to the average customer.** They have the most to gain, they forgive a rough product, and they want to give feedback. They also make a negative result hard to explain away: if even they do not come back, the strategy has a problem. Check: are the users this experiment reaches the ones who feel the problem most sharply, and if they turn it down, will the result be read as a refutation? Sources: Ries, Introduction (opening); chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down).

## In front of customers

**A minimum viable product judged only by its makers has not been tested; it is put in front of the customers it is for, sometimes even offered for sale, to see how they react.** The makers' view of whether it is good says nothing of its effect on the people it is for. Check: who outside the team will use this, and how will their reaction be captured? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering).

## The concierge

**Before anything is automated, the first customers are served by hand and in person, doing what the product would do, one at a time, and automation begins only when serving them by hand leaves no room for the next.** Ries calls this the concierge minimum viable product. Judged by traditional measures it is hopelessly inefficient, but each round teaches what the product requires, and what they actually do is measured on a real service before any system exists to deliver it. When automation comes, it scales something that works rather than inventing something that might. Check: what would the first users get if the founder did by hand what this work would automate, and has the manual version actually run out of room? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down); chapter 6, Test (The concierge minimum viable product).

## The manual version is not the product

**In a concierge, the personal service is a way of testing the leaps of faith, not the product, and a common result is that it disproves the planned model of growth even while the customers it serves are satisfied.** A manual version can work well for a few customers and say nothing about whether the model holds beyond them. Mistaking it for success is the trap of the small, satisfied business that should have pivoted. Check: which leap does the manual version test, and what would scaling it assume that the manual version does not test? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (The concierge minimum viable product).

## Fake the hard part

**Where a hard technical problem stands between an idea and its test, the hard part is faked: in a Wizard of Oz test, customers believe they are using the real product while people do the work behind it.** It is inefficient and would lose money at scale, but on a small scale it answers the question that comes first: if the hard problem were solved, would people use the product and get value from it? Leaving the hard problems unsolved early lets idea after idea be rejected cheaply, and the one that engages comes with a map of what to build. Check: is a hard technical problem being solved before anyone has shown they would use what it enables, and could a person do that part by hand for now? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (The Village Laundry Service); chapter 6, Test (Pay no attention to the eight people behind the curtain).

## The cheap version of a request

**Where customers ask for something costly, the cheapest version that meets the need behind the request is tried first, because customers care whether it serves them, not how long it took to build.** A crude shortcut can beat a faithful, expensive implementation, and even the features the makers were proudest of. Check: what is the cheapest thing that would meet the need behind this request, and has it been tried? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (The role of quality and design in an MVP).

## Everything the customer meets is the product

**The product is everything a customer experiences in dealing with the maker, not only the software, so the manual parts of an experiment are part of what is tested.** Ries uses the broadest definition of a product, any source of value for the people who become customers, whether a store, a website or a service. A message sent by hand, a setup done in person, or an explanation given beside the screen is part of what the customer is responding to. Check: what does the user meet in this experiment besides the screen, and is that counted as part of what the result measures? Sources: Ries, chapter 2, Define (If I'm an entrepreneur, what's a startup?).

## Quality is the customer's to define

**If the customer is not known, quality is not known, so a first version is held to professional standards only where they serve its customers: what it leaves out is finish, and what it holds is built soundly.** Ries shipped a first product full of bugs that nobody got far enough into to find, because the value proposition was what failed; a version customers find low in quality also teaches which qualities they care about, and sometimes they love it as it is. Where a defect stands between early adopters and the benefit being tested, it spoils the experiment, since their reaction is then to the defect rather than the value. Rough means left out, never built badly: finish and polish no user has asked for wait, while a defect that would keep users from the value or a result from being read is fixed. Check: is this standard being applied because a user's behavior asked for it, and does any known defect stand between the users of this experiment and the value it tests? Sources: Ries, Introduction (opening); chapter 3, Learn (Launch); chapter 6, Test (The role of quality and design in an MVP); chapter 11, Adapt (Can you go too fast?).

## Tested before the vision is whole

**The fear that a vision will be judged wrong before it has had a real chance pushes testing back until the vision is built in full, and that raises the very risk it fears: by then too little is left to pivot with.** Makers with a vision fear a false negative, a flawed or too-small first version rejected by customers. The answer is to face the fear and test early, willing to fail, even in public. Check: is the test being held back until more of the vision is built, and what would be lost by testing the part that exists? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (Speed bumps in building an MVP); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Pivots require courage).

## Fear is no reason to hold a release

**A release that would reach its customers is not held back for fear of what they will think of its quality, or for fear that the idea will be stolen or a brand damaged, because the delay costs the feedback the release was for.** As launch neared, Ries's team was afraid the product would damage their names; they shipped anyway, because building something nobody wants was the worse outcome. Established companies already have more ideas than they can use, a head start is rarely large enough to matter, and a new product's obscurity is an advantage; brand damage comes from loud launches that overpromise, and a quiet release avoids it. Check: is this release waiting on something a user hit, or on fear of what users or outsiders will think? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Six months to launch); chapter 6, Test (Speed bumps in building an MVP).

## The whiteboard cannot find the error

**Most errors in a plan lie in its facts, not its strategy, and more analysis cannot find them, because they depend on how the product and its customers interact; building after a few cursory conversations fails the other way.** One danger is building at once, reading customers who do not yet know what they want as agreement; the other is analysis paralysis, refining the plan without end. What ends the analysis is a minimum viable product. Check: is more planning being spent on a question only a user can answer, and is enough known to say what the first test is? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Analysis paralysis).

## Large changes are tested too

**The smallest version comes first, but fine-tuning reaches diminishing returns: where small changes have stopped moving the measure, a larger change can itself be the experiment, read against the version it would replace.** Too much focus on low-risk, low-yield tuning stops the large changes a product sometimes needs. A large redesign usually does worse at first, and is improved against the old version until it does better. Check: have small changes stopped moving the measure, and if a large change is proposed, what will it be read against? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Failure to pivot).

## Fresh users for each change

**Where each change is to be judged on its own, a small, steady stream of new users gives every change its own report card, since each day's newcomers are independent of the day before.** The point is learning, not growth: a few new customers a day, at trivial cost, are enough to see each step customers take through the product, while the gross numbers can rise all the while the steps do not change. Check: will the users who meet this change be new to the product, so that what they do can be credited to it? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Improving a product on five dollars a day).

## An experiment is a first product

**A successful experiment is the first version of the product: it comes with its first customers, and its feedback becomes the specification for what is built next.** Ries's example of a hotline is a seed that grows into the full service, one kind of request at a time. The specification that results rests on what works today rather than on guesses about what might work tomorrow. Check: if this experiment succeeds, what part of it carries forward as the product, and what does its feedback say the next build is? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (An experiment is a product; A Lean Startup in government?).
