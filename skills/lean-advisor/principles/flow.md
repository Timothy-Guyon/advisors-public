# Flow: how does work move, and what is fixed, how far?

The claims about how work moves through the loop: what counts as waste, how large a batch is, what pulls work, when work is done, and how much goes into fixing what goes wrong.

## What the learning did not need is waste

**In a new product, value is validated learning about what customers want, so any effort that did not contribute to that learning is waste, however well it was done.** Ries had followed the best development methods of his day and still wasted most of his team's effort, because those methods define value as benefit to a known customer, and a new product does not yet know its customer. The rule reaches past features to any process or effort: debate over features customers never reached, and integrations beyond the one that would have taught the same thing, were waste. Check: which part of this work would the open experiment's learning have needed, and which part would it have done without? Sources: Ries, Part Three, Accelerate (Start your engines); chapter 3, Learn (Throwing my work away; Value vs. waste); chapter 6, Test (Why first products aren't meant to be perfect); chapter 9, Batch (Small batches in entrepreneurship).

## Minimize time through the loop

**The aim is to minimize the total time through the build-measure-learn loop, and every process is judged by how much it shortens that loop, not by how fast any one step runs or how busy anyone is.** Professional training tends to perfect one step: engineers learn to build as efficiently as possible, and large batches feel efficient to the individual expert. Lean methods deliberately suboptimize single steps for the loop, and a process that does not shorten it is overhead. Check: does this shorten the time from a change to a user's reaction written down, or improve one step while the loop waits? Sources: Ries, Introduction (The Lean Startup method); chapter 2, Define (A seven-thousand-person Lean Startup); Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 9, Batch (Continuous deployment beyond software; The large-batch death spiral); chapter 12, Innovate (Becoming the status quo).

## Small batches find problems sooner

**Working in small batches, one piece carried all the way through before the next, reveals defects, and that customers do not want the product, far sooner than large batches do.** Ries takes single-piece flow from lean manufacturing: the large batch looks efficient but hides the cost of piling, moving and reworking half-finished work, and finds the bad letter or the broken seal only at the end. For a new product the problem found late is that nobody wants it, and a small batch spends the least time, money and effort before finding out. Check: what is the smallest piece of this that can reach a user whole, before the next piece starts? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (opening; Small batches in entrepreneurship; Small batches at IMVU).

## The large-batch death spiral

**Large batches grow on their own: the longer since the last release, the more is feared from customers' reaction, and the more one more feature or fix seems worth adding before shipping.** Ries calls it a death spiral because, unlike in a factory, nothing physical caps the size of the batch; the release becomes a do-or-die version, and the more work is done, the more remains. Fear of the customers' verdict grows with the batch, and the batch grows with the fear. Check: how long since a user last saw a change, and is the work being added now making the next release easier to ship or harder? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (The large-batch death spiral).

## Get data now

**Postponing data until success feels certain increases wasted work and the risk of building what nobody wants, and an experiment can start right away, alongside any planning still under way.** Ries names the trap the audacity of zero: zero invites imagination, small numbers invite questions, so the pull is to stay at zero. Planning takes months; an experiment on the plan's assumptions can begin at once and inform the plan as it forms. Check: what is this work postponing data for, and could a test of the same assumption begin now? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (The audacity of zero); chapter 4, Experiment (For long-term change, experiment immediately; Break it down).

## Everything unshipped is inventory

**Everything made before a customer uses it, designs, specifications, untested assumptions and plans as much as code, is work-in-progress inventory.** A new product cannot see its inventory, because it is intangible: until the minimum viable product ships, all the work in it is inventory, and nearly every lean technique works by turning push into pull and shrinking batches, both of which reduce it. Inventory that is never used is pure waste. Check: how much of what exists for this product, in any form, has no user used yet? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (Pull, don't push).

## The hypothesis pulls the work

**Work is pulled by a hypothesis about customers that needs testing, not by customers' requests and not by what could be built; any other work is waste.** Ries rejects reading pull as building what customers ask for, since customers often do not know what they want; the signal is the experiment that has to run, built in the smallest batch that does the job. Check: which hypothesis pulls this work, and if a user asked for it, which hypothesis does the request bear on? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (Pull, don't push; Hypothesis pull in clean tech).

## A gap found at the desk is a spare part

**An analysis of the product's own model shows what the product can and cannot do, not what its users need, so each gap it finds is work kept in stock just in case until the experiment under way pulls it.** Ries's picture is the parts warehouse: mass production avoids a stockout by holding spares of every model just in case, and lean production makes one more only when one is used. A map of every gap, each filled, is that warehouse: it says where the product would fail a user, and only how users meet the product says which of those places they reach, an error no amount of desk analysis can find. The order in which gaps are filled comes from the hypothesis under test, not from the map. Check: which experiment's question does filling this gap answer, has any user met it, and is any of this work filling a gap nobody has reached? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (Pull, don't push); chapter 5, Leap (Analysis paralysis).

## Done means validated

**Work is done when it is validated, known to have been a good idea or not, not when it is built, and a limit on the work in each state means new work starts only once earlier work has been validated.** Teams used to counting delivered features feel stuck at first, because the only way to start more is to find out whether the finished work mattered, often by talking to customers; soon they build validation in from the start. Keeping each part busy, the builders above all, can undermine the whole. Check: has the work already built been validated, and should anything new start before it is? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Actionable metrics versus vanity metrics; Kanban).

## Remove what fails validation

**Where validation shows a piece of work was a bad idea, it comes out of the product.** Work that changes nothing customers do is not harmless left in: it carries its cost forward in everything built after it. Check: what in the product has been shown to change nothing for users, and is it coming out? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Kanban; Hypothesis testing at Grockit).

## Detail does not rescue a plan

**When results do not come, specifying the next iteration in more detail makes things worse: planning slows, batches grow, and feedback is delayed.** The fault blamed on builders not working hard enough usually lies in a way of deciding that cannot tell whether any change mattered. Check: is the answer to a disappointing result more specification, or a test of the plan behind it? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Optimization versus learning).

## Learning makes the next test faster

**Successive experiments get faster because of what was learned about customers, the market and the strategy, not because of code accumulated along the way; much of what is built is discarded at a pivot, and what remains is a legacy that takes work to reform.** Reusable infrastructure is a weaker reason for speed than it looks. Check: is this being built for the direction now under test, or carried from a direction the record has already left? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots).

## Just in time, not ahead of demand

**Infrastructure, scale and process are built when learning shows they are needed, never in advance of success, because capacity built for demand that has not appeared commits the product to a forecast.** Ries's example executed its launch plan faithfully, invested for the millions it expected, and could not change course when they did not come. He frames the dilemma as two failures, overarchitecture that delays any product and collapse just when adoption takes off, and answers it with just-in-time scalability: experiment without large investment up front, and add scale and quality as each need appears. Check: what observed need does this infrastructure meet now, and what event would show it is needed if it is not built yet? Sources: Ries, chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup); Part Three, Accelerate (Start your engines); chapter 11, Adapt (opening; Can you go too fast?).

## Too much is as harmful as too little

**A new product is harmed by too many resources as much as by too few: what it needs is little, held securely.** It needs far less than an established business but is acutely sensitive to having that changed midway, and excess has sunk as many ventures as scarcity. Abundance removes the constraint that forces the smallest experiment. Check: is the ease of building this standing in for the constraint that would have forced a smaller version? Sources: Ries, chapter 12, Innovate (Scarce but secure resources).

## Tooling earns its time by shrinking the batch

**An investment in tools or process pays when it shrinks the batch, the distance from a change to a customer using it.** Ries's examples, from die changes cut from hours to minutes to a way of letting real customers run a new version safely beside the old, all count their benefit in smaller batches, not in faster individual work. Enabling small batches can take real investment, and the batch it shrinks is what justifies it. Check: does this tool bring a change to a user sooner, or only make the work at the desk faster? Sources: Ries, chapter 9, Batch (opening; Small batches in education); chapter 11, Adapt (Year three: explosion).

## Defects that slow the loop

**Sloppiness is not the price of speed: a defect that slows the loop itself, keeping a user from the product, a result from being read, or the product from being changed, is fixed when it is felt, in proportion to what it cost.** Defects cause rework, low morale and complaints that slow everything after them, so speed bought with sloppiness is false. Guarding against every imaginable failure is the opposite waste: speed comes from skipping the work that does not lead to learning, not from skimping on the work that does. Check: has this defect slowed the loop, or is the work meant to prevent a failure no user has met? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (The role of quality and design in an MVP); chapter 11, Adapt (Can you go too fast?); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (In conclusion).

## Stop the line

**Where a defect reaches a customer, its fix and the search for its cause go ahead of new work on what it broke, so that nothing new is built on top of it; work elsewhere goes on.** This is the andon cord: any worker may halt the line at a defect, and in Ries's continuous deployment a change that harms customers is pulled, the team is told, and nothing new goes out until the cause is fixed. Stopping costs flow now and saves far more later, since defects compound. Even the smallest venture serves the customers it already has while it innovates, so keeping the product running for them is a standing part of the work, not a detour from it. Check: is any user's use broken right now, and is anything being built on top of what broke before it is fixed? Sources: Ries, chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup); chapter 9, Batch (opening; Small batches at IMVU); chapter 11, Adapt (Can you go too fast?).

## Five whys to the root

**After any failure, a technical fault, a missed result or an unexpected change in customer behavior, the question why is asked until the human or process cause behind it appears.** Ries adapts Ohno's five whys: the visible fault is a symptom, and the root nearly always lies in how the work is done, so fixing only the symptom lets it recur. Run at every failure, it ties the rate of progress to learning rather than to execution alone. Check: for this failure, what chain of causes leads from the symptom to how the work is done, and which link is being fixed? Sources: Ries, chapter 11, Adapt (The wisdom of the Five Whys; Automatic speed regulator).

## Proportional investment

**Prevention is bought in proportion to the pain of the symptom: a small investment for a minor problem, more only if it recurs.** The five whys make a small investment at each level of the cause, so prevention grows only where problems keep costing time, and the method works as an automatic speed regulator. Where a whole team cannot be convened, Ries gives two rules for one's own work: tolerate every mistake the first time, and never allow the same mistake twice. Check: how painful has this problem been, and is the prevention proposed no larger than that pain? Sources: Ries, chapter 11, Adapt (Make a proportional investment; Automatic speed regulator; Getting started; Facing unpleasant truths).

## A trigger is simple and ironclad

**The rule that says when a problem calls for root-cause work is narrow, simple and unambiguous, so that anyone can tell when it has fired.** Ries advises starting with one specific class of symptoms and a rule that admits no argument, keeping each session short and its changes small, and widening only as the practice settles. An ambiguous trigger is never sure to fire and invites dispute each time. Check: could two people disagree about whether this trigger has fired? Sources: Ries, chapter 11, Adapt (Start small, be specific).

## Old problems are fixed as they recur

**Long-standing problems are not swept up in a campaign; they are fixed a little at a time when they come up in new failures, and one that never comes up may not be as big as it seems.** Ries saw an attempt to push years of baggage through root-cause analysis at once fail under its own weight. Endemic problems surface on their own in the analysis of new ones, and that is when they are fixed. Check: has this old problem caused a new failure, or is it being fixed because it is known to exist? Sources: Ries, chapter 11, Adapt (Don't send your baggage through the Five Whys process).

## Fix the method, not the effort

**Recurring problems come from the method, not from the people, and neither exhortation to try harder nor a decree of a new target changes the outcome; changing how the work is done does.** Ries draws this from three places: the five blames, where finger-pointing replaces the root cause and the answer is to ask what made the mistake so easy; the decree of a shorter cycle that left the old system and its results intact; and Deming's teaching that what matters is the method by which goals are reached. Check: is the response to this problem a change in how the work is done, or a resolution to try harder at the same thing? Sources: Ries, chapter 11, Adapt (The curse of the Five Blames; Year two: muscle memory); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (opening).
