# Decisions: pivot, persevere or stop, and when is that decided?

The claims about the decision an experiment's result leads to: what a pivot is, what the decision hears, when it is made, and what does not count in it.

## Steer, do not launch a rocket

**Under extreme uncertainty, a new product is steered by continual adjustment through the build-measure-learn loop instead of by executing a detailed plan, because forecasting works only with a long, stable history.** Ries contrasts a car, steered moment to moment, with a rocket, whose every step is set before launch so that a small error in the assumptions becomes a catastrophe far downrange. Each turn of the loop decides whether to make a sharp turn, a pivot, or to persevere. Check: does this plan fix steps and results in advance, or does it name the next action and the result that decides the one after? Sources: Ries, Introduction (Why startups fail); chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup); chapter 4, Experiment (A Lean Startup in government?).

## Tune the product, pivot the strategy, keep the vision

**The product changes constantly through tuning, the strategy changes less often through a pivot, and the vision rarely changes at all.** Ries layers a new venture as vision, then strategy, its business model, road map and idea of who the customer is, then the product that results from the strategy. A setback is a chance to learn how to reach the destination, not a reason to drop it. Check: does this result call for tuning the product or changing the strategy, or does it really reach the vision? Sources: Ries, chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup).

## What a pivot is

**A pivot is a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the business model or the engine of growth; it is not a synonym for change, and it needs a new minimum viable product to test it.** Tuning the product toward the same hypothesis is persevering; a pivot changes the hypothesis. Nor is a pivot an exhortation to change: it is the structured change that lets a wrong turn be noticed and another path found. Check: does this change test a new fundamental hypothesis, and what experiment will test it? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (opening; A catalog of pivots; A pivot is a strategic hypothesis).

## Name the kind of pivot

**Pivots come in kinds, each named for what it changes: a feature becomes the whole product, or the product becomes one feature; a different customer segment, a different customer need; an application becomes a platform, or the reverse; the business architecture, the way value is captured, the engine of growth, the channel, the technology.** Naming the kind says what changes and what stays, so the new hypothesis is stated precisely. Check: what does this pivot change, and what does it hold the same? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (A catalog of pivots).

## Judgment, sharpened by tests

**No formula makes the decision to pivot or persevere; vision, intuition and judgment stay in it, and the method's part is to improve that judgment by testing it again and again.** People are so good at seeing signals in noise that they see some that are not there, and repeated tests correct for it. Check: is the decision being presented as following mechanically from the numbers, or as a judgment informed by them? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (opening).

## A decision that cannot drift

**The decision to pivot or persevere is made at a point fixed before the experiment opens: its closing event, and a limit counted in its users' uses at which it closes even if its result never comes.** The decision is emotionally charged, so it is made in a structured way, set in advance: not so soon that the experiment cannot report, not so late that a failing direction runs on. Ries holds the meeting on a rhythm of weeks to months and caps each experiment's length and reach, so that a weak one ends rather than drifts; counting the limit in uses keeps it an event rather than a date. Check: what event closes this experiment, and what count of its users' uses closes it if that event never comes? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (The pivot or persevere meeting); chapter 12, Innovate (Creating an innovation sandbox).

## Data in the room

**The decision hears the complete record of experiments over time, each set against the prediction made for it, with detailed accounts of what current and potential customers said, and an outside view to question it.** Learning milestones do not make the decision easy; they make sure the data is in the room when it is made. After many conversations with customers there is always enough learning to rationalize a failure, and results set against predictions made in advance are what stop it. The outside view is there because makers read their own data through their preconceptions. Check: are the whole record against its predictions, and what users said, in front of this decision, and who is there to question it? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots; The pivot or persevere meeting).

## Asked about before it is decided

**A disappointing number calls first for asking the experiment's own participants why, and for investigating alternatives with them and with those the plan assumed would resist, so that a failing direction comes with evidence of where to turn.** An early experiment already supplies the people to talk to and a record of what they did, so no new survey is needed. Data alone can show that a strategy is not working without showing what would. Where a core assumption is refuted outright, or the people the strategy depends on cannot be found at all, it is time to pivot. Check: when the number misses, have the users who took part been asked why, and does the miss refute a core assumption or only the current version? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Launch); chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (The pivot or persevere meeting).

## When to consider a pivot

**The signs that call for a pivot are experiments growing less effective, and a product that improves version after version while customers' behavior does not change; both can appear while the gross numbers still rise.** Ries shipped fixes and features daily for months while purchases stayed near zero, and what moved the numbers was a change of strategy. Tuning that reaches diminishing returns, many individually successful experiments that together barely move the drivers, means the direction is spent, not the effort. Success hides it: once a direction works, advice turns to staying the course. Check: are the recent experiments moving the measure less than the ones before, and has the product improved across several versions while what users do has not? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Launch; Where do you find validation?); chapter 7, Measure (Pivot or persevere); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (The pivot or persevere meeting; Failure to pivot).

## How a pivot is judged

**A pivot has succeeded when the experiments after it are more productive than those before it: the same effort now moves the drivers.** After a pivot the cycle starts over, with a new baseline and tuning from there. Check: since the change of direction, are experiments moving users' behavior more than they did before it? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (How innovation accounting works—three learning milestones; Cohort analysis).

## The living dead

**The most dangerous result is moderate success, enough to stay alive and not enough to meet expectations: a product neither growing nor dying, consuming effort, kept going out of loyalty and the myth of perseverance.** Most products, even failing ones, have some customers and some growth, and optimism keeps belief alive when the evidence says otherwise. The stories told are of those who persevered and won, never of those who persevered too long. Check: is a modest positive result being read as a reason to continue, without the prediction it was meant to meet? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (opening); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (opening; Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots).

## Commit to iterate

**A commitment to iterate is made before the first test: a failed first test is grounds to pivot or persevere, and stopping is for a vision whose pivots no longer teach anything.** Minimum viable products often bring bad news, and teams trained to make a go or kill decision at each stage give up when a first version fails. The vision is tested as fast as possible, but it is not abandoned as fast as possible: what is needed is perseverance and flexibility together, with a pivot when an element of the strategy proves flawed. Check: before this test runs, is it agreed what a bad result leads to, other than giving up? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (Speed bumps in building an MVP; From the MVP to innovation accounting); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (In conclusion).

## Built work is not evidence

**Where a result refutes the strategy, the work built for that strategy is thrown away however good it is, and the builder's reluctance is no evidence for keeping it.** Ries was the last at IMVU to admit the strategy was wrong, because months of his own code had to be thrown out; efficient methods had let him build, efficiently, a product customers refused to use. The consolation that the work taught something is true only to the extent the lesson could not have been learned more cheaply. Check: at this decision, does any part of the case to persevere rest on the work already built rather than on what users did? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Launch; Throwing my work away).

## Keep what was learned

**A pivot keeps one foot in what has been learned and repurposes what customers already use; it does not throw everything out and start over.** Even a dramatic change of name, positioning and customers can keep, at its core, the most valuable work done so far. Check: which learning, and which parts users already used, carry into the new direction? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots; The pivot or persevere meeting).

## Decide while it is cheap

**The more time, money and creative energy sunk into an idea before it is tested, the harder and dearer the pivot, so testing early with little invested brings the decision while it is still cheap to make.** Facing the decision a few months in, rather than after a full build, leaves room to act on it; large early investments in facilities or scale make every pivot slower. The size of what is built before the test sets the price of being wrong. Check: if this hypothesis is disproved, how much built work goes with it, and could it meet its first decision with less built? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots); chapter 9, Batch (Hypothesis pull in clean tech).

## Runway in pivots

**A new product's true runway is the number of pivots it can still make, not the time or money left; it is extended by reaching each pivot faster, and cutting what keeps the loop moving only fails more slowly.** Every technique of the method has the same aim: the same validated learning at lower cost or in less time. Check: does this bring the next decision to pivot or persevere nearer, or push it away? Sources: Ries, chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (A startup's runway is the number of pivots it can still make).

## Product/market fit does not end pivots

**Where a team has to ask whether it has product/market fit, it does not have it yet, and neither a pivot nor fit is a final state.** Ries rejects two readings of fit: that a pivot is a failure to find it, and that once it is found, pivots stop. Each engine of growth has measures that show whether the product is approaching fit, read through innovation accounting. Check: is this decision treating a pivot as a failure, or fit as an end to pivoting, rather than reading the engine's measure? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (Engines of growth determine product/market fit).

## An experiment never postpones a decision

**An experiment is run to discover the truth, never as a stalling tactic or as make-work that puts off a hard decision.** Badly run experiments give experimentation a bad name, because running one comes to mean postponing a decision; assumptions are stated and tested out of a wish to learn, not to delay. An experiment whose every outcome leaves the decision where it was is a delay dressed as rigor. Check: what decision does this experiment move, and is it being run because the decision is hard to make? Sources: Ries, chapter 12, Innovate (Protecting the parent organization); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (In conclusion).

## Test ideas, do not sell them

**One experiment is open at a time, and inside it a disagreement the next users can settle is settled by what they do, not argued.** Ries quotes Intuit's founder: with one test a year you get politicians who sell ideas, and with hundreds you get people who run, learn and retest. Where only one test can run, the selling replaces the learning; where tests are cheap and fast, ideas are tested rather than argued. This holds for a founder working alone as much as for a team, wherever a disagreement would otherwise be settled by argument. Check: is this disagreement something the next users could settle faster than the argument about it, without changing what the open experiment measures? Sources: Ries, Introduction (The Lean Startup method); chapter 2, Define (A seven-thousand-person Lean Startup).
