# Learning: what counts as learning, and how is it measured?

The claims about what progress is, which evidence shows it, and how a result is read so that it teaches something.

## Learning is the unit of progress

**A new product's progress is measured in validated learning about what customers want, by learning milestones, not by work completed to plan, on time and on budget.** If the product is something nobody wants, delivering it well does not matter: executing a flawed plan faithfully is what Ries calls achieving failure, and a process that hears from customers only at the end guarantees it. Asked whether the product is getting better, teams answer with the changes shipped and the numbers that rose; that is storytelling, since it shows neither that the changes caused the results nor that the right lessons were drawn. Check: what did users do differently because of this work, and is that what is being reported as progress? Sources: Ries, Introduction (The Lean Startup method); chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup); chapter 3, Learn (opening; Value vs. waste); Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 7, Measure (Why something as seemingly dull as accounting will change your life); chapter 11, Adapt (Year one: achieving failure; Year three: explosion).

## A learning claim needs its evidence

**A claim to have learned a lot is the oldest excuse for a failure of execution; validated learning is shown only when real customers' behavior improves in the next cycle, after the product changes on the strength of it.** Ries admits that his own account of what IMVU learned was a story with no evidence until later versions did better with real customers. Anyone can tell a good story about what a failure taught them, and a failure justified as learning is pseudoscience unless the next experiment demonstrates it. Check: what learning is claimed here that has not yet changed what a user does, and what in the next experiment will show it? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (opening; Value vs. waste; Where do you find validation?); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (Product development pseudoscience).

## What customers do, not what they say

**A leap of faith is validated by what customers do, signing up, paying, coming back, not by what they say in a survey or a focus group, by an analogy, or by a statement of intent.** Most people cannot say in advance what they want, least of all about something unfamiliar, and the makers' own model of how customers behave can be years out of date. Even a small real test sets a baseline of behavior closer to the truth than research about what people should want, and an organization eager enough to sign a letter of intent can still fail to buy. Check: is this result something users did, or something they said they would do? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Launch; Talking to customers; Value vs. waste); chapter 4, Experiment (Think big, start small; Break it down); chapter 6, Test (The video minimum viable product; The concierge minimum viable product); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Innovation accounting leads to faster pivots).

## A voluntary exchange of value

**The test of the value hypothesis is a voluntary exchange of value between customers and the product, and the exchange need not be money: time and attention given freely count, and coming back of their own accord is the clearest sign.** Ries reads value from retention, how many of the first participants return, because giving time and attention to something unprompted is strong evidence it is worth something to them; one use proves less than a second. Payment shows value too, but where charging would choke adoption, sustained voluntary use is the exchange that matters. Check: what are users in this experiment giving the product of their own time, attention or money without being asked, and how many came back unprompted? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down); chapter 10, Grow (The viral engine of growth).

## Start from a baseline

**The first learning milestone is a baseline: a minimum viable product establishes real data on where the product stands now, however far from the ideal, and later experiments are read against it.** The job is first to measure where things really are and face what that shows, then to devise experiments that move the real numbers toward the plan. A baseline is valuable even when it is very bad news. Check: what is the current number, measured from real users, that this experiment is meant to move? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (opening; How innovation accounting works—three learning milestones; Establish the baseline).

## Aim at a driver

**Every initiative aims at one driver of the model and is judged by whether it moved it: a change of design that does not improve the behavior it targeted has failed, because a good design is one that changes customer behavior for the better.** The effort spent and the makers' confidence count for nothing in that judgment. Check: which user behavior is this meant to change, and what result would count it as failed? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Tuning the engine).

## Cause and effect must be readable

**A result teaches only if its cause can be read: one measure is held through the experiment, and changes go out so that each one's effect can be told apart.** Shipping several changes at once and celebrating any rise, or watching a different number each cycle, leaves numbers that rise and fall seemingly on their own. Working out afterwards which change did what takes long enough that the questions have moved on by the time it is answered. Check: when the result comes in, will it be possible to say which change caused it? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Tuning the engine; Actionable metrics versus vanity metrics).

## Direction and degree, not size

**A product's progress is judged by the direction and degree of change in its actionable measure across trips through the loop, not by the size of the number today.** A product with a smaller but rising rate is making real progress, while one with a larger flat rate is stuck. Each turn of the loop is read for whether the work moved customer behavior, and the raw total says nothing about that. Check: across the last trips through the loop, did the measure move, in which direction, and because of which change? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (Engines of growth determine product/market fit).

## Vanity metrics

**A metric is actionable when it shows clear cause and effect; gross totals such as cumulative customers, visits or overall revenue are vanity metrics, which give the rosiest picture and say nothing about whether the product is improving.** Vanity metrics prey on a weakness of the mind: when the numbers rise, people credit whatever they were working on, and when they fall, someone else is to blame. Check: does this number show what users did because of a change, or does it only go up? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Optimization versus learning; Vanity metrics: a word of caution; Actionable).

## Growth can hide that the work changes nothing

**Rising totals can come entirely from customers already arriving, while the work on the product has no effect on what they do.** Vanity metrics and traditional accounting let a team believe its work improves the product when the numbers rise on their own, until the source of new customers runs out and a crisis follows. Only a measure that ties a change to a change in behavior shows the work did anything. Check: would this number have moved anyway, without the change being credited for it? Sources: Ries, chapter 10, Grow (When engines run out).

## Success theater

**Work done to look successful is success theater, not progress: buying a bump in the totals, selecting the data that supports the vision, staying out of sight of customers so that there is no data at all, or claiming cause and effect by marking events on a graph of totals.** Ries names the temptation for a new product whose small numbers cost it confidence: the bump passes and the resources are gone, and the fundamentals win out in the end. All innovation begins with a vision; what matters is exposing it to a test it could fail. Check: is the evidence offered for this the whole result of a test that could have failed, or a selection, an absence, or a highlight on a total? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (The audacity of zero); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (Product development pseudoscience).

## Accessible reports

**A report is accessible when the people deciding can understand it: simple, in concrete units of people and what they did, and in front of everyone who needs it.** Metrics are people: anyone can picture a person who used the product, and nobody is sure what a hit is. A report that turns complex actions into counts of people and the behaviors that matter is the gold standard. Check: is this result stated as which users did what, in words that can be read at a glance? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Get out of the building); chapter 7, Measure (Accessible).

## Auditable data

**Data is auditable: credible to the people it judges, checkable by hand against real customers, and produced by means simple enough to trust, drawn from the source rather than from a system in between.** When a result would kill a pet project, the first defense is to challenge the data; spot checks with customers answer the challenge and also show why they behave as the data says. A judgment overturned by a fault in the data costs confidence and discipline. Check: can each number in this result be traced by hand to a user and what they did? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Auditable).

## Cohorts

**Progress is read by cohort analysis: each group of customers who meet the product in a period is followed on its own, as the share who go on to each behavior that matters, rather than as cumulative totals.** Each group is an independent report card, so a failure cannot be blamed on earlier customers or on outside conditions. Sequences of customer behavior predict far more than gross numbers do. Check: is the result read for the users who met this version, apart from those who met earlier ones? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Cohort analysis; Accessible).

## Split tests

**Where enough customers arrive to divide them, a split test offers different versions at the same time to comparable customers who do not choose which they see, and reads cause from the difference in what each group does.** Splitting at the same moment keeps everything but the change equal. Ries's first example of validated learning is such a test of how the product was described. Split tests routinely show that features the makers believe improve the product change nothing, which saves the time spent on work customers do not care about and can question the belief behind a whole line of features. Check: where this experiment compares two versions, did comparable users see them at the same time, or one after the other while other things changed too? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Where do you find validation?); chapter 7, Measure (Cohorts and split-tests; Hypothesis testing at Grockit).

## A handful can be decisive

**A small sample gives a decisive answer when the result is extreme: if every one of a handful of early adopters declines, that is a highly significant negative result.** Ries designs early tests on a dozen people and still reads them as evidence, because the prediction is stated in advance and the people chosen have the most to gain. A small number of users is not a reason to wait for more before reading the result. Check: does the prediction name a result that the handful of users in this experiment could give clearly enough to settle it? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down).

## Whose reaction counts

**Whose reaction counts is settled before the result: the customers the product is for, not commentators outside the target however loud, and not only those who liked it.** Before data, it is easy to dismiss a customer who dislikes the product as outside the target and keep recruiting until one agrees. After a launch, it is easy to be swayed by public criticism from people the product was not built for, while its own users respond quite differently. Check: are the users whose reaction will judge this named before it runs, and is any reaction being set aside after the fact? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Cohort analysis); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (Pivots require courage).

## A number and a conversation

**A good experiment gives a clear, countable outcome and also puts its makers face to face with customers, and a number that will not move gives those conversations their urgent questions.** Serving real customers exposes problems no plan would think to ask about; the number says whether something happened, and talking to the people involved says why and what to ask next. Before the numbers, conversations tend to confirm what the makers already believe; with a failure declared, it is known what to look for. Check: besides its one number, does this experiment put its makers where users can surprise them, and what question has the latest result raised for them? Sources: Ries, chapter 3, Learn (Launch); chapter 4, Experiment (Think big, start small; The Village Laundry Service); chapter 7, Measure (Cohort analysis).

## Go and see

**Customers are understood firsthand: no part of a problem is understood without going to see it, rather than relying on others' reports, and the people who build the product watch customers use it.** The facts about customers, markets and channels exist only outside the building. Builders who watch users can change the product on the spot, and they feel the pain of a user not knowing what to do. Check: has someone who builds this watched a user use it, or is the account second-hand? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Genchi gembutsu; Get out of the building); chapter 6, Test (Pay no attention to the eight people behind the curtain).

## Feedback is input, not instruction

**What customers say is one source of information, not a set of orders: the job is a synthesis between the vision and what customers will accept, neither giving in to what they say they want nor telling them what they ought to want.** Ries talked to his early adopters constantly and did not do what they said; he was far more likely to run an experiment than to grant a request. A request shows where to look, and behavior settles the question. Check: is this being done because a user asked for it, or because a hypothesis about what users will do calls for it? Sources: Ries, Introduction (opening); chapter 3, Learn (opening; Where do you find validation?).

## What early users ask for, and what nobody misses

**Where the users of a stripped-down version ask for a missing feature, that is early evidence the feature matters; a planned feature nobody misses may matter less than the plan said.** Ries reads complaints about missing features as a sign the team is on the right track, and silence about a feature on the road map as a reason to doubt it. Both readings come from users meeting the reduced product, not from the road map. Check: which parts of the plan have users asked for after using the reduced version, and which has nobody missed? Sources: Ries, chapter 4, Experiment (An experiment is a product).

## Optimization is not learning

**If the product is the wrong one, optimizing it, its performance, its ease of use, its marketing, yields nothing significant, and most ideas for improving a product make a difference only at the margins.** In an established business, hard work on a sound plan yields incremental gains; a new product must clear a higher bar, evidence that a sustainable business can be built around it, and that takes the large experiments that change what is believed. A team can improve the product every week for months while customer behavior does not move, and the usual problem is not effort or talent but a plan, executed with discipline, that does not make sense. Check: if this were perfected, which user behavior would change, and has the hypothesis behind it been shown? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Innovation accounting at IMVU; Optimization versus learning); chapter 10, Grow (The three engines of growth).

## A productive day is not progress

**A day of uninterrupted building feels productive and proves nothing; working toward learning feels less efficient than working in large batches, and that feeling is to be expected, not taken as evidence.** Ries, as a programmer, counted a good day as eight hours of code with no interruptions, because code is tangible and learning is not. He predicts that anyone who moves to learning milestones will feel less productive and ask to go back to big batches. Check: is this work favored because it makes for a productive-feeling day, or because a user will act on it? Sources: Ries, chapter 1, Start (The roots of the Lean Startup).
