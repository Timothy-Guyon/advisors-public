# Assumptions: what is being assumed, and which assumption is tested first?

The claims about what a plan takes for granted, which of its assumptions could sink it, and the order they are tested in.

## The method is for extreme uncertainty

**Where success depends only on execution, because the customer, the product and the model are already known, plain execution is enough; the method exists for work under extreme uncertainty.** Ries defines a startup by the uncertainty it works in, not by its size or its sector: a copy of a proven business is not one, since its outcome can be forecast. What is uncertain has to be tested, and what is known only has to be done. Check: is this work uncertain about what a user will do, or only about how to carry it out? Sources: Ries, chapter 2, Define (If I'm an entrepreneur, what's a startup?).

## Should before can

**The open question for a new product is whether it should be built, not whether it can be, so the assumptions most likely to sink it are about customers, not about the technology.** Productive capacity now outruns the ability to know what to build, and the waste in innovation comes from building the wrong thing efficiently. Ries spent years treating product failures as technical problems, and each technical fix brought more failure. The common error is to ask whether a solution can be built before asking whether customers recognize the problem, would take up a solution, and would take it from this maker. Check: of the four questions, does the user recognize the problem, would they take up a solution, would they take it from this maker, and can it be built, does this work answer any but the last? Sources: Ries, Introduction (Origins of the Lean Startup); chapter 3, Learn (Lessons beyond IMVU); chapter 4, Experiment (An experiment is a product); chapter 13, Epilogue: Waste Not (opening).

## A few leaps among many facts

**Most of a plan's assumptions are facts already known; a handful are leaps of faith on which the whole venture rests, and each stays an assumption until it has been tested on real customers, even when research supports it.** The mundane assumptions come from past experience or plain deduction and need no test. The leaps are the ones that take courage to say in the present tense, that customers want this or that a partner will carry it, and they hide among the facts. If one is false, the venture fails outright. Check: of everything this plan assumes, which assumptions would sink it if false, and has a test on real users settled any of them? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 3, Learn (Lessons beyond IMVU); chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down); chapter 5, Leap (Strategy is based on assumptions).

## Value and growth are the two leaps

**Two leaps of faith stand above the rest, the value hypothesis, whether customers find the product valuable once they use it, and the growth hypothesis, how new customers will find it, and the first step is to learn whether the product creates value at all.** Ries breaks every vision into these two first and gives each its own test of behavior; every other assumption is judged by which of the two it serves. He speaks of value rather than profit, because a venture can be profitable for a while and still destroy value. Check: which of the two does this experiment test, and if it is about growth, has value been shown first? Sources: Ries, Part Two, Steer (How vision leads to steering); chapter 4, Experiment (Break it down); chapter 5, Leap (opening; Value and growth).

## Riskiest assumption first

**The riskiest assumption is tested first, because if it cannot be moved toward what a sustainable business needs, testing the others is pointless.** An experiment can test several assumptions at once or one at a time; either way the order follows the risk. Where the answer to one question is already well known, the first experiments go to the other. Check: is this experiment aimed at the assumption most likely to fail, or at a safer one whose answer is already known? Sources: Ries, chapter 7, Measure (Establish the baseline).

## A problem worth solving

**The first leap to confirm is that the customer has a problem significant enough to be worth solving.** Early conversations ask whether people feel the problem, not which features of a solution they would want: customers who have never seen a kind of solution cannot yet have an opinion about it. Check: has anyone confirmed, from users' own account of what they do today, that they have this problem and that it matters to them? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Get out of the building).

## Analogy hides the leap

**An argument by analogy to an earlier success hides the leap of faith; restated plainly, as what customers want right now and why they cannot get it, the leap shows, and it calls for a test before anything is built.** Analogies are used to make a venture look less risky, and they invite building the technology first to see whether customers come. Used honestly, analogs settle the questions others have already answered, and antilogs, where others failed, expose the questions still open; the open ones are the leaps. Check: stated without the comparison to another product, what does this assume users want now, and which part of it has no analog to answer it? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Strategy is based on assumptions; Analogs and antilogs).

## Borrowed success is a hypothesis

**A technique, a best practice or a strategy that worked for another company is a hypothesis for this one, to be tested in its own circumstances.** Every technique in a famous story has counterexamples, and success stories are told around the final strategy, not the pivots that found it, so it is hard to know whether the essential features were copied or only the superficial ones. Even an industry's best practice can turn out, when tested, to change nothing customers do. Check: is this being done because it worked elsewhere, and what would show that it works here? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (opening); chapter 7, Measure (Hypothesis testing at Grockit); chapter 8, Pivot (or Persevere) (A pivot is a strategic hypothesis).

## Early contact finds the questions

**Early contact with customers does not give definitive answers; it shows, at a coarse level, who the customer is and which assumptions most need testing.** A plan's first version is guided by hunch, and that is right; getting out of the building is how hunches become data. Unlike an established company improving a known product, a new product learns from early contact what to test, not what to build. Check: is a conversation with users being read as an answer, or as the source of the next question to test? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Value and growth; Genchi gembutsu; Design and the customer archetype).

## The archetype is provisional

**The description of the target customer is a hypothesis, and it stays provisional until validated learning shows that this kind of customer can be served sustainably.** A short description of the customer, the archetype, guides daily priorities, but treating it as finished, the way a design agency treats its one deliverable, stops the learning just when it is needed. No amount of design can anticipate how a product will meet the real world. Check: is a description of who the user is being treated as settled, and what has any user done that supports it? Sources: Ries, chapter 5, Leap (Design and the customer archetype).

## The leap as a number

**Each leap of faith is written as a number the plan depends on, in a model of the business however rough, before it is tested.** A share of customers expected to sign up, buried in one cell of a spreadsheet, is a leap and is written as one, plainly and where it cannot be missed. Innovation accounting begins by turning the leaps into such a model, even one on the back of a napkin, so that progress can later be read against it. Before value is shown, the numbers are about use, and the money in the model waits. Check: what number does this plan depend on, and is it written down as an assumption before the test? Sources: Ries, chapter 6, Test (Why first products aren't meant to be perfect); chapter 7, Measure (An accountability framework that works across industries).
